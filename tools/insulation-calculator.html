Blown-In Insulation Calculator

Blown-in insulation is the easiest way to top up an under-insulated attic, but coverage math is confusing: bags are rated in cubic feet, R-value depends on depth, and the blower machine's coverage chart rules the job.

How This Calculator Works

Bags = (Area ÷ 1,000) × ~27 bags × (Depth ÷ 14 in)   |   R ≈ Depth × 2.8–3.7 per inch
  1. Determine target depth: existing insulation + added depth must reach your R-goal (R-38 minimum in most US attics, R-49–R-60 recommended in cold climates).
  2. Estimate bags: a common cellulose product blows about 20–35 bags per 1,000 sq ft at R-38 (≈10–14 in settled).
  3. Scale bags by depth relative to the 14-inch benchmark.
  4. Estimate the R-value achieved: cellulose ≈ R-3.7 per inch, blown fiberglass ≈ R-2.2–3.2 per inch.
  5. Depth rulers: place a yardstick every ~300 sq ft so you can verify depth while blowing.

Example: A 1,000 sq ft attic topped to 14 in of cellulose: about 28 bags, ≈R-49 after settling, roughly $225–$550 in material.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many inches of blown-in insulation equals R-38?

For cellulose (R-3.7/inch), R-38 takes about 10–11 inches settled; for blown fiberglass (R-2.2–3.2/inch), expect 12–16 inches. Always use the settled depth from the bag's coverage chart — loose-blown depth fluffs up 15–20% and settles.

How much does blown-in insulation cost in 2026?

Bags of cellulose run roughly $15–$25 each (a 25 lb Greenfiber bag is about $16–$20 at big-box stores; premium lines reach ~$37). DIY material for a 1,000 sq ft attic at R-49 costs about $420–$700, with the blower machine usually free with a minimum bag purchase. Professional installation adds roughly $1–$2.50 per sq ft.

Can I blow insulation over existing insulation?

Yes — it's one of the best ROI projects in a home. Check that existing insulation is dry and unfaced (don't cover a vapor barrier with new fill), air-seal penetrations first, and keep insulation off recessed light fixtures and soffit vents using baffles.

How deep should attic insulation be for my climate?

US DOE guidance: R-38–R-49 for warm climates (zones 1–3), R-49–R-60 for most of the country, and R-60 for cold zone 7–8. In cellulose terms that's roughly 11–16 inches. Existing depth counts — measure and add what's missing.

Cellulose or blown fiberglass — which is better?

Cellulose (ground paper, borate-treated) settles denser: better R-per-inch, better air sealing, but heavier on ceilings. Blown fiberglass is lighter and itchier but slightly lower R per inch. Both cost similar per achieved R; pick on price and availability locally.
